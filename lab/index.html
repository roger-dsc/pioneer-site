Log

    Sensors on every run see the trend days early. Depot checks find it only if one falls between P and F. Every 30 days, a trackside detector finds it hot, with passengers aboard. Illustrative: the fleet and its faults are made up.

    Set the alarm

    Set it low and faults are caught early, but healthy bearings trip it too. Ask for readings in a row and the noise cancels, but the warning comes later.

    What is watched
    Axle bearings
    Overheat, then seize.Temperature and vibration sensors on the bogie as the train runs; heat and acoustic detectors beside the track. Microphones can hear damage inside a bearing, often well before it is hot enough to set off a temperature alarm19.
    Wheels
    Flat spots, out of round, a worn profile.Detectors built into the track measure how hard each wheel hits the rail; lasers and cameras measure its shape and wear19. Worn wheels are turned back to shape at the depot.
    Bogie frame
    Fatigue cracks.Ultrasonic testing at the bogie and general inspections; and, in Japan since 2017, air-spring pressures and bogie temperatures watched for change as the train runs22.
    Pantograph
    A worn carbon strip.Cameras and sensors at the depot entrance inspect the outside of every car as it rolls in3; on the board, a strip is changed at 5 mm.
    Traction
    A converter fails.Built so one failure is not a stop: a Velaro D has four independent converters and still reaches its destination on 75% of its power if one fails7.

    Five answers

    Reliability-centred maintenance gives each way a part can fail one of five answers25:

    Predictive
    Watch the condition and act on the trend: the bearing sensors on the board.
    Preventive
    Restore or replace on a schedule, by time or distance, where a part wears out at a predictable age.
    Detective
    Test what only fails when it is needed, such as a standby system, before it is needed.
    Run to failure
    Let it fail, where failure is cheap and safe: a reading light.
    One-time change
    Change the design when no task can make it safe. After Eschede, every wheel of the failed design was replaced with a one-piece wheel within weeks24.

    What is changing
    Prediction, not only detection
    Prognostics estimates how long a part has left, its remaining useful life, as a range that narrows as readings come in. Its methods learn from data, model the physics of the damage, or combine both26. On the board, with a reading on every run, the dashed line ahead of the bearing is that estimate.
    Sensors that power themselves
    Wireless sensors on the bogie run on the train’s own vibration, with no batteries or wiring, so an older fleet can be watched on every run too. Hitachi Rail says parts replaced on condition last more than 25% longer, and that none of the 3,000-plus carriages fitted has had a critical component fail in service27.
    Thousands of sensors a train
    China’s Fuxing trains carry more than 2,500 sensors, watching bearing temperatures, brakes and the cabin; when something is wrong, the train can slow down or stop by itself28. The smart Fuxing built for the 2022 Winter Olympics has 2,70029.
    Compared with its neighbours
    A reading means more beside others taken at the same moment. Norfolk Southern’s detectors raise an alert for a bearing much warmer than the others on the same side of the train, or on the same car, even below the absolute limit23. The board compares each bearing with its pair on the axle.
    Every train an inspection train
    Doctor Yellow, the Shinkansen’s yellow inspection train, measured the track and overhead wire at up to 270 km/h32. JR Central retired its set in January 2025; the work is moving to sensors and cameras on ordinary N700S trains43.
    Robots under the train
    At Nanjing South, robots with two camera arms scan a high-speed train’s underside, and AI flags what they find. By hand, a standard train needs at least two inspectors for two and a half hours; with the robots, an hour and a ten-minute review30. At Zhanjiang North depot, a robot arm tests hollow axles by ultrasound for cracks of a millimetre31.

    Did you know
    • Reliability-centred maintenance began with airlines: a 1978 report by United Airlines engineers for the US Department of Defense. Its starting point was that the old idea of a fixed safe life for every part was wrong in nearly every example in a modern jet airliner25.
    • Vibration analysis can find a defect in a bearing’s inner race about 12 weeks before the bearing seizes; check it every 6 weeks or more often20.
    • In December 2017 the crew of Nozomi 34 noticed an unusual smell and noise from under the floor soon after leaving Hakata. The train ran on for over three hours; at Nagoya, staff found a crack 146 mm deep in a bogie side beam 170 mm high, with about 1,000 passengers aboard22.
    • On that train’s previous run, trackside detectors had recorded the bogie warmer than usual, but under the alarm level; and the air-spring pressures logged on the train show the crack was probably weakening the frame the day before22.
    • Afterwards JR Central went from two trackside bogie-temperature detectors between Tokyo and Shin-Osaka to five, and began judging each bogie by how its temperature changes, not only by a limit. JR West told its staff: do not hesitate to stop the train when safety cannot be confirmed22.
    • In February 2023, three trackside detectors read a freight car’s bearing at 38, then 103, then 253 °F above the air (about 21, 57 and 141 °C) in 75 minutes. Only the last crossed the 170 °F level at which a detector radios the crew; the train derailed minutes later at East Palestine, Ohio23.
    • The NTSB found that a failing bearing is likely hotter inside than a trackside detector can measure from outside23.
    • Before the Eschede derailment in 1998, which killed 101 people, the ICE’s wheel tyres were inspected by eye with torches; testing machines used earlier had been dropped for raising too many false alarms. In the two months before, staff filed eight complaints about noise and vibration from the bogie24.
    • In China, an eight-car high-speed train is inspected every three days in service, a check of more than 10,000 components30.

    What makes the speed possible.

    The train

    Nose
    A long single arch keeps the air attached and the tunnel pressure gentle.
    Body
    Aluminium and composite: light on the axles, still stiff.
    Seal
    Sealed and pressure-managed: a tunnel is a change of air, not a thump.
    Bogies
    Welded frames, air springs and anti-roll bars: a steady, low ride.
    Traction
    25 kV, silicon-carbide converters and permanent-magnet motors: more speed, less weight and heat.
    Braking
    Motors return braking energy to the line; an eddy-current brake and carbon-ceramic discs, tested for the CR450, take the rest16.
    Suspension
    Active dampers cancel what the track sends up.
    Tilt
    Air springs lean the body slightly to hold speed through curves.

    The line

    Alignment
    Curves gentle enough to stay comfortable at design speed.
    Slab track
    Concrete slab: little upkeep, no ballast to fly.
    Viaducts
    The line stays level; the ground below stays open.
    Tunnels
    Shaped portals keep the pressure wave gentle.
    Catenary
    Tensioned wire keeps the pantograph in contact in the wind.
    Switches
    High-speed turnouts, with no jolt at the crossing.
    Fencing
    Fenced, no level crossings: nothing else shares the track.

    The systems

    Signalling
    ETCS Level 2: movement authority by radio, the permitted speed in the cab.
    Automatic driving
    Holds the timetable to the second; brakes earlier and softer.
    Interlocking
    Proves each route clear and locked before a train moves.
    Radio
    A dedicated network for signalling and voice.
    Traffic management
    Re-plans around a delay and gives the driver a new target.
    Monitoring
    Sensors report wear before it becomes a failure.
    Power supply
    Substations and auto-transformers hold the voltage as trains accelerate.

    In service on the N700S in Japan8, the TGV M in France9 and the Velaro in Europe7; China’s CR450, still in testing, brings several of them to 400 km/h6.

    How it works

    Plain physics, not a simulation of a real train: each run pulls away as hard as grip and power allow, holds top speed, and brakes to the platform.

    From the sources

    • Power: set so an eight-car train reaches 350 km/h in 4 min 40 s, as the CR450 does5. Longer trains carry the same share of powered cars.
    • Curves: banked by up to 180 mm, with up to 100 mm left unbalanced above 300 km/h10.
    • Fixed blocks: braking runs over four blocks11.
    • CO₂e per passenger-km: car 167 g, flight 229 g, international rail 4.5 g13. Construction and maintenance: 58 t of CO₂ per km a year15.
    • Checks: more often than the time from P to F, usually every half of it21.

    Assumed

    • Starting acceleration 0.45 m/s²; service braking 0.55 m/s².
    • Air resistance from a drag area of 2.3 m² plus 0.65 m² a car; rolling resistance 0.15% of the weight.
    • Eight cars: 550 seats and 470 t; twelve: 830 and 690 t; sixteen: 1,100 and 920 t. Three seats in four taken, 80 kg a passenger.
    • 85% of the energy from the wire reaches the wheels; 60% of the braking energy goes back; 0.4 MW for lights, air and doors.
    • Three minutes at each stop, fifteen to turn round; spares 15% of the trains in service; trains from 06:00 to midnight.
    • A grid at 250 g of CO₂ per kWh; an average trip of 110 km.
    • Moving block keeps 200 m behind the train ahead.
    • The health board: one bearing, on car 3 of train P04, starts to fail on day 8, its reading growing with the square of the days since. It can be seen at 6 °C above its pair (P), and a trackside detector trips at 30 °C (F). With sensors on every run it is watched from 6 °C and repaired at 10 °C; a depot check that finds it at 6 °C or more repairs it that night.
    • The prediction: a curve (a quadratic) fitted to the last seven readings while the bearing is failing, extended to 30 °C; its range is where the curve, with twice its standard error, crosses 30 °C.
    • The alarm: a healthy bearing reads 2 °C above its pair, give or take 2 °C, with each day’s reading independent of the last; 64 bearings a train, one reading each a day.
